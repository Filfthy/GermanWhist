The aim

Win more of the last 13 tricks than your opponent. You score a point for every trick you take above six.

The deal and trumps

A standard 52 card deck is used. Each player is dealt 13 cards, and the rest form the stock. The top card of the stock is turned face up: it is the first prize, and its suit is trump for the whole game.

Playing a trick

One player leads a card and the other follows. You must follow suit if you can; if you can't, you may play any card. The highest card of the suit led wins, unless a trump is played, in which case the highest trump wins. The winner leads the next trick.

Round 1: drafting

The winner of each trick takes the face up prize. The loser takes the top card of the stock, unseen by the other player. A new prize is then turned up. These 13 tricks don't score: you are building your hand for Round 2.

Round 2: scoring

When the stock runs out, play the 13 cards you have built. Only these tricks count.

Dealer and matches

The First deal setting decides who deals the first game. The non-dealer leads the first trick. In a match the deal alternates, and the first to 10 points wins.

A little history

Despite its name, there's nothing German about German Whist. It's a two-player version of Whist and is most likely British. It's also known as Honeymoon Whist and Chinese Whist, and in Sweden as Hamburger Whist.

Whist itself grew out of an older English game, Ruff and Honours, and became hugely popular in the 18th century after Edmond Hoyle published his short treatise on it in 1742. Whist needs four players; German Whist's drafting round, where the stock is fought over trick by trick, lets two people play.

Players have long disagreed about the scoring. Some count all 26 tricks; this game, like many players, counts only the 13 tricks of Round 2, which makes Round 1 purely about building your hand.

Most good card games need three or four people, and this one needs only two. Now you know how to play, why not get out a real deck and play it with someone across the table?

Tips
  • In Round 1 you aren't just trying to win tricks: you're building a strong hand for Round 2.
  • If the prize is poor, think about losing the trick: the stock card might be better.
  • Keep track of the trumps and high cards that have gone.
  • When it's your turn your name glows and the cards you can play rise from your hand.
